POKÉMON POWER CREEP ANALYSIS: A STATISTICAL INVESTIGATION USING GENERALIZED LINEAR MODELS

Author: Jan Tychtl
Course: SAN (Statistická Analýza)
Date: December 2025

================================================================================
ABSTRACT
================================================================================

This report investigates the phenomenon of "power creep" in the Pokémon franchise — the tendency for later-generation Pokémon to have systematically higher combat statistics than earlier ones. Using a dataset of 1,024 Pokémon across 9 generations, we applied OLS regression, Generalized Linear Models, LASSO regularization, and various cross-validation techniques.

Our analysis reveals moderate-to-strong evidence for power creep. Generation is a statistically significant predictor of Attack (Likelihood Ratio test p = 0.0077), and this effect is robust to outlier removal (p = 0.002). Importantly, power creep persists among Normal-rarity Pokémon (correlation = 0.732), confirming the trend is not driven by legendary inflation. However, the effect size is modest — approximately 1-2 attack points per generation — and the pattern is not perfectly monotonic, with Generation 2 and Generation 6 showing lower attack than Generation 1.

================================================================================
1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1 Background and Research Question

Power creep is a well-documented phenomenon in game design where newer content tends to be stronger than older content. In the Pokémon franchise, this would manifest as later-generation Pokémon having systematically higher combat statistics. Our central research question is: Does generation significantly predict Pokémon strength after controlling for other factors?

1.2 Data Overview

We analyzed three datasets derived from a comprehensive Pokémon database:

    • Full Dataset: 1,024 Pokémon across 9 generations
    • Outlier-Free Dataset: Approximately 950 Pokémon with statistical outliers removed
    • Normal-Only Dataset: 934 Pokémon excluding legendaries and mythicals

The primary target variable is the Attack stat, a continuous measure ranging from 5 to 181. Generation sizes vary considerably, from 72 Pokémon in Generation 6 to 156 in Generation 5.

================================================================================
2. EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS
================================================================================

2.1 Attack Distribution Across Generations

Our initial exploration revealed that mean attack ranges from 68.3 in Generation 2 to 84.8 in Generation 7 — a difference of over 16 points. However, this pattern is not monotonic. Generation 2 and Generation 6 actually have lower mean attack than Generation 1, creating a non-linear trajectory that complicates simple power creep narratives.

When we examine the Normal-only dataset, the same pattern persists with means ranging from 66.3 to 81.4. This is our first indication that power creep exists independently of legendary Pokémon.

{IMAGE: 2.1-ATTACK_BOXPLOT_THREE_DATASETS}

2.2 The Shape of Distributions Changes

Beyond means, we observed that the shape of attack distributions evolves across generations. Generation 1 shows an approximately normal distribution centered around 70-75 attack. By Generation 7-9, distributions have become right-skewed with increasingly heavy tails — more Pokémon with above-average attack and fewer with below-average attack.

This shift in distribution shape is consistent with power creep affecting not just the average but the entire statistical profile of newer generations.

{IMAGE: 2.2-ATTACK_HISTOGRAM_BY_GENERATION}

2.3 Correlation Structure

Attack correlates moderately with other combat stats: HP (r = 0.42), Defense (r = 0.44), and Speed (r = 0.38). These correlations remain stable across generations, suggesting the fundamental relationships between stats haven't changed — Pokémon are simply becoming stronger across the board.

Importantly, we found a negative correlation between Attack and Special Attack (r = -0.14), indicating a trade-off between physical and special offense. This specialization pattern persists across all generations.

{IMAGE: 2.3-CORRELATION_MATRIX}


================================================================================
3. THE RARITY CONFOUND
================================================================================

3.1 Legendary Pokémon Are Stronger

As expected, rarity strongly predicts Attack. Normal Pokémon average 75.1 Attack, while SubLegendaries average 100.5, Legendaries 114.8, and Mythicals 99.4. This 25-40 point difference could potentially explain apparent power creep if later generations simply contain more legendary Pokémon.

3.2 But Rarity Distribution Differs by Generation

A chi-square test confirms that rarity distribution differs significantly across generations (p = 5.07 × 10⁻⁸). Most notably, Generation 7 contains 18 rare Pokémon out of 88 total (20.5%), compared to an average of approximately 9% across all generations.

{IMAGE: 3.2-RARITY_COMPOSITION_BY_GENERATION}

3.3 Controlling for Rarity: The Normal-Only Analysis

To address this confound, we analyzed only Normal-rarity Pokémon. The correlation between generation and mean attack is 0.732 for Normal Pokémon compared to 0.757 for the full dataset. This near-identical correlation is strong evidence that power creep is a baseline phenomenon affecting all Pokémon, not just legendaries.


================================================================================
4. LINEAR REGRESSION ANALYSIS
================================================================================

4.1 Basic Model: Attack Predicted by Generation Alone

Our simplest model regresses Attack on Generation treated as a categorical variable. The results are sobering: R² = 0.032. Generation alone explains only 3.2% of the variance in Attack. Nevertheless, the F-test confirms generation is statistically significant (p < 0.001).

The Normal-only model produces nearly identical results (R² = 0.031), reinforcing that power creep exists among regular Pokémon.

4.2 Full Model with Controls

Adding control variables dramatically improves fit. The model:

    Attack ~ Generation + HP + Defense + Sp.Attack + Sp.Defense + Speed + 
             Height + Weight + Rarity + Abilities + Evolution Length

achieves R² = 0.473. After controlling for these factors, specific generation effects emerge more clearly. Compared to Generation 1:

    • Generations 2 and 6 have LOWER attack (approximately -4 to -5 points)
    • Generations 4, 5, 7, 8, and 9 have HIGHER attack (+4 to +8 points)
    • Generation 7 shows the largest positive effect (+8.5 points)

Variance Inflation Factors are all below 2.3, indicating no problematic multicollinearity.

4.3 The Interaction Question

We tested whether the generation effect varies by rarity using interaction terms (Generation × Rarity). The interaction model does not significantly improve over the additive model (p = 0.33), meaning power creep affects all rarity categories similarly. This is an important finding: legendaries are not experiencing different power creep than normal Pokémon.


================================================================================
5. REGULARIZATION AND FEATURE SELECTION
================================================================================

5.1 LASSO Feature Selection

Using 10-fold cross-validated LASSO regression (α = 1), we identified which features survive regularization. Out of 20+ candidate predictors, LASSO selected 17 with non-zero coefficients. Crucially, multiple generation dummies were retained, confirming generation has predictive value beyond what other variables explain.

The strongest predictors retained by LASSO were Defense (+12.6), HP (+9.15), and Speed (+8.72), followed by generation effects.

5.2 Backward Stepwise Selection

AIC-based backward selection produced a parsimonious model:

    Attack ~ Generation + HP + Defense + Sp.Defense + Speed + log(Weight) + Rarity

Variables dropped as non-significant included Sp.Attack (redundant with the attack specialization pattern), Height, Number of Abilities, Evolution Length, and Capture Rate. Importantly, Generation survived selection, confirming its predictive relevance.


================================================================================
6. GENERALIZED LINEAR MODELS
================================================================================

6.1 Gaussian GLM

A Gaussian GLM with identity link produces results identical to OLS regression. This model serves as our primary specification given that Attack is approximately normally distributed.

6.2 Gamma GLM for Positive Skewed Data

We also fitted a Gamma GLM with log link to handle potential heteroscedasticity and the strictly positive nature of Attack. Results were qualitatively similar, though time-split cross-validation showed worse out-of-sample performance (RMSE 30 vs. 24 for OLS).

6.3 Poisson GLM for Count Outcomes

As a secondary analysis, we modeled Number of Abilities using Poisson regression. Interestingly, later generations have FEWER abilities than Generation 1 — the opposite of power creep for this metric. The mean abilities count is 2.37 with variance 0.55, indicating underdispersion rather than overdispersion.


================================================================================
7. FORMAL HYPOTHESIS TESTING
================================================================================

7.1 Likelihood Ratio Test

The central statistical test for our research question is whether Generation significantly improves model fit after controlling for other variables. Comparing full and reduced GLMs:

    Full Dataset:       χ² test, p = 0.0077
    Outlier-Free Data:  χ² test, p = 0.0020

Both p-values are below 0.05, providing statistically significant evidence that Generation predicts Attack beyond what other variables explain. Notably, the effect is STRONGER after removing outliers — outliers were dampening, not inflating, the power creep signal.

7.2 Jonckheere-Terpstra Test for Monotonic Trend

This non-parametric test specifically examines whether there is an ordered increase in Attack across generations. Results:

    Full Dataset:       p = 2.09 × 10⁻⁵
    Outlier-Free Data:  p = 1.61 × 10⁻⁵

Both are highly significant, confirming a monotonically increasing trend. Despite the dips in Generations 2 and 6, the overall trajectory is upward.


================================================================================
8. CROSS-VALIDATION ANALYSIS
================================================================================

8.1 Ten-Fold Cross-Validation Ablation Study

We compared nine models using 10-fold CV:

    1. Null Model (intercept only):     RMSE ≈ 29, R² ≈ 0
    2. Generation Only:                  RMSE ≈ 28, R² ≈ 0.03
    3. Basic Stats (Gen + HP/Def/Speed): RMSE ≈ 22, R² ≈ 0.45
    4. Full Linear Model:                RMSE ≈ 21, R² ≈ 0.50
    5. GLM with Splines:                 RMSE ≈ 21, R² ≈ 0.51 (BEST)

The spline model achieved the best performance, though improvement over the linear model was modest. The key insight is that Generation-only barely beats the null model — combat stats drive most predictive power.

{IMAGE: 8.1-MODEL_COMPARISON_DOTPLOT}

8.2 Time-Split Cross-Validation

A more stringent test trains on Generations 1-5 and predicts Generations 6-9. Results:

    OLS Model:    RMSE = 24, R² = 0.42
    Gamma GLM:    RMSE = 30, R² = 0.35

Performance degrades from 10-fold CV (~21 RMSE) to time-split (~24 RMSE), suggesting that future generations don't perfectly follow past patterns. This is a caveat for the power creep claim — the effect exists but isn't perfectly predictable.

{IMAGE: 8.2-TIME_SPLIT_PREDICTED_VS_ACTUAL}


================================================================================
9. TYPE-SPECIFIC POWER CREEP
================================================================================

9.1 Do Different Types Experience Different Power Creep?

Using LASSO with Generation × Type interaction terms, we tested whether power creep varies by Pokémon type. Out of 18 possible type interactions, LASSO selected only 4:

    • Normal types (+0.39): Stronger-than-average power creep
    • Flying types (+0.02): Slightly stronger power creep
    • Poison types (-0.42): Weaker-than-average power creep
    • Fairy types (-0.13): Weaker power creep

9.2 Type-Specific Correlations

Examining correlations between generation and mean attack by type reveals substantial variation:

    Strongest power creep (correlation > 0.5):
        Fighting (0.76), Ice (0.68), Grass (0.62), Fairy (0.60)
    
    Weakest or reverse trends (correlation < 0):
        Rock (-0.20), Dragon (-0.18), Psychic (-0.09), Fire (-0.004)

Notably, Dragon-type — often considered a "power" type — shows slight negative correlation. Early-generation Dragons like Dragonite set a high baseline that later generations haven't systematically exceeded.

{IMAGE: 9.2-TYPE_TRENDS_FACETED}

9.3 ANOVA Test for Overall Type Interactions

Despite these individual patterns, an ANOVA test shows that adding all 17 type interaction terms does NOT significantly improve model fit (F = 0.998, p = 0.457). Power creep is relatively uniform across types, with only minor type-specific deviations.


================================================================================
10. RARITY-SPECIFIC POWER CREEP
================================================================================

10.1 A Striking Pattern

The most surprising finding emerges from rarity-specific analysis. Correlations between generation and attack:

    Normal:        +0.73 (strong power creep)
    SubLegendary:  +0.71 (strong power creep)
    Legendary:     +0.09 (essentially flat)
    Mythical:      -0.16 (slight decrease)

Legendaries and Mythicals are NOT getting stronger. Power creep is concentrated among Normal and SubLegendary categories.

10.2 SubLegendary Specialization

Even more striking: SubLegendary attack correlates positively with generation (+0.71), but total stats correlates NEGATIVELY (-0.64). Newer SubLegendaries are becoming more offense-specialized — higher attack but lower total stats. They're becoming glass cannons rather than balanced powerhouses.

{IMAGE: 10.2-RARITY_TRENDS_ATTACK_AND_TOTAL}


================================================================================
11. ROBUSTNESS CHECKS
================================================================================

11.1 Outlier Analysis

Approximately 74 Pokémon (7%) were identified as statistical outliers and removed. Key findings:

    • Correlation (gen vs. attack) changes from 0.757 to 0.725 after removal
    • LR test p-value improves from 0.0077 to 0.0020
    • JT test p-value improves from 2.09×10⁻⁵ to 1.61×10⁻⁵

All effects are STRONGER without outliers. This is critical: outliers were dampening the power creep signal, not creating it.

11.2 Robust Regression

Huber M-estimation, which downweights influential observations, produces coefficients nearly identical to OLS. The maximum difference for any generation coefficient is 0.24 points. This confirms outliers are not driving our conclusions.

11.3 Influential Observations

Using Cook's Distance, we identified the most influential individual Pokémon:

    • Deoxys-Attack (Gen 3): 180 Attack, Cook's D = 0.089
    • Kartana (Gen 7): 181 Attack, Cook's D = 0.076
    • Rampardos (Gen 4): 165 Attack, Cook's D = 0.052

Even these most influential points have Cook's D well below standard thresholds (0.5 or 1.0). No single Pokémon is driving the power creep finding.


================================================================================
12. EFFECT SIZE CONSIDERATIONS
================================================================================

While power creep is statistically significant, practical magnitude matters. Key effect sizes:

    Cohen's d (Gen 7 vs. Gen 1):  0.41 (small-to-medium)
    Cohen's d (Gen 9 vs. Gen 1):  0.35 (small)
    η² for generation:            0.024 (small)
    η² for Defense:               0.131 (large)
    η² for HP:                    0.150 (large)

Combat stats (HP, Defense) have LARGE effects on Attack. Generation has a SMALL but statistically significant effect. The practical interpretation is that power creep exists but is subtle — a Gen 9 Pokémon has perhaps a 10-15 point Attack advantage over an equivalent Gen 1 Pokémon, which matters in competitive battling but is modest compared to within-generation variation (standard deviation ≈ 28).


================================================================================
13. CONCLUSIONS
================================================================================

13.1 Evidence FOR Power Creep

Our analysis provides moderate-to-strong evidence for power creep in the Pokémon franchise:

    1. Statistical Significance: Likelihood Ratio tests confirm generation is a 
       significant predictor (p = 0.0077 full, p = 0.002 outlier-free) even after 
       controlling for HP, Defense, Speed, Rarity, and other factors.

    2. Monotonic Trend: The Jonckheere-Terpstra test confirms a significant 
       increasing trend (p = 2.09 × 10⁻⁵).

    3. Robust to Outliers: Effects are STRONGER after outlier removal.

    4. Not Driven by Legendaries: Normal-only correlation (0.732) nearly matches 
       full data (0.757). Power creep affects regular Pokémon.

    5. Broad-Based: Total stats also increase with generation (cor = 0.843).

    6. Type-Uniform: LASSO selected only 4/18 type interactions; ANOVA p = 0.46.

13.2 Caveats and Limitations

    1. Non-Monotonic Pattern: Generations 2 and 6 dip below Generation 1.
    
    2. Small Effect Size: Approximately 1-2 attack points per generation.
    
    3. Mediation: Much of the effect is mediated by other combat stats.
    
    4. Time-Split Performance: Prediction degrades for future generations.
    
    5. Single Dimension: Attack is one aspect of competitive viability; speed, 
       typing, abilities, and movepool also matter.

13.3 Final Verdict

MODERATE-TO-STRONG EVIDENCE FOR POWER CREEP

Later-generation Pokémon are systematically stronger than earlier ones, and this trend is robust across multiple statistical approaches, datasets, and specifications. However, the effect is modest in magnitude and not perfectly linear. Power creep is real but subtle — a gradual upward drift rather than dramatic inflation.

The most interesting secondary finding is that power creep is concentrated among Normal and SubLegendary Pokémon. Legendaries and Mythicals are NOT getting stronger, suggesting game designers are intentionally maintaining balance at the highest tiers while allowing baseline inflation.

APPENDIX: METHODS SUMMARY
================================================================================

Statistical methods employed in this analysis:

    • OLS Regression: Baseline linear models
    • Generalized Linear Models: Gaussian, Gamma, Poisson families
    • LASSO/Ridge/Elastic Net: Regularized regression for feature selection
    • Backward Stepwise Selection: AIC-based model simplification
    • Likelihood Ratio Tests: Formal hypothesis testing
    • Jonckheere-Terpstra Test: Non-parametric monotonic trend test
    • Huber M-Estimation: Robust regression
    • 10-Fold Cross-Validation: Model comparison
    • Time-Split Cross-Validation: Generalization testing

Software: R with packages tidyverse, glmnet, car, MASS, broom, caret, lmtest, 
AER, rcompanion, DescTools.
