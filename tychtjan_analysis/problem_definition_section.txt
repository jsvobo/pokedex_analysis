================================================================================
PROBLEM DEFINITION AND RESEARCH FRAMEWORK
================================================================================


THE CHALLENGE OF DEFINING "POWER CREEP"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Power creep is a colloquial term from gaming communities describing the tendency for newer content to be stronger than older content. However, this concept is inherently vague:

    • What constitutes "power"? Attack? Defense? Speed? Some combination?
    • Does power creep mean higher averages, or more extreme outliers?
    • Is a 5% increase meaningful, or do we need 20%?
    • Does power creep affect all Pokémon equally, or only certain types/rarities?

Rather than chase this imprecise definition, we reframe our research question more broadly.


OUR RESEARCH QUESTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

    "Is there a significant difference between earlier and later generations 
     of Pokémon according to Pokédex data?"

This formulation is deliberately broader than "power creep" because:

    1. We search for ANY significant change, not just positive trends
    2. We make no prior assumption about the direction of change
    3. We let the data reveal what kind of generational differences exist

If we find significant positive trends in combat-relevant statistics, we can then interpret this as evidence of power creep. But we remain open to finding no effect, negative trends, or complex non-monotonic patterns.


THE NULL HYPOTHESIS FRAMEWORK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We approach this analysis in the spirit of hypothesis testing:

    Null Hypothesis (H₀):
    "Generation has no effect on Pokémon statistics. If we order Pokémon 
     by generation, there is no systematic difference between earlier 
     and later releases."

    Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):
    "Generation has a significant effect on Pokémon statistics. Later 
     generations are systematically different from earlier ones."

Our task is to find sufficient statistical evidence to reject H₀. We do NOT assume power creep exists — we assume the opposite and try to disprove it.


GENERATION AS A PROXY FOR TIME
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Pokémon franchise releases new creatures in distinct "generations" tied to major game releases:

    Generation 1 (1996): Red/Blue         - 151 Pokémon
    Generation 2 (1999): Gold/Silver      - 100 new Pokémon
    Generation 3 (2002): Ruby/Sapphire    - 135 new Pokémon
    Generation 4 (2006): Diamond/Pearl    - 107 new Pokémon
    Generation 5 (2010): Black/White      - 156 new Pokémon
    Generation 6 (2013): X/Y              - 72 new Pokémon
    Generation 7 (2016): Sun/Moon         - 88 new Pokémon
    Generation 8 (2019): Sword/Shield     - 96 new Pokémon
    Generation 9 (2022): Scarlet/Violet   - 119 new Pokémon

Generation serves as an ordinal proxy for time — Gen 1 Pokémon were designed in the mid-1990s, while Gen 9 Pokémon were designed in the early 2020s. Any generational differences reflect nearly 30 years of evolving game design philosophy.


================================================================================
WHY ATTACK AS THE TARGET VARIABLE?
================================================================================

To test for generational differences, we need a concrete target variable. We select ATTACK as our primary proxy for "power." This choice requires justification.


THE CASE FOR ATTACK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Attack directly measures damage-dealing capability
   → A Pokémon with 150 Attack hits harder than one with 50 Attack
   → This is the most intuitive interpretation of "power"

2. Attack is relevant regardless of other stats
   → A Pokémon can still be powerful with high Attack and low Defense
   → It becomes a "glass cannon" — fragile but deadly
   → High Attack alone can win battles through overwhelming offense

3. Attack is universal across all Pokémon
   → Every Pokémon has an Attack stat (unlike type-specific moves)
   → Allows direct comparison across the entire dataset

4. Attack captures offensive design intent
   → Designers who want to create "powerful" Pokémon tend to give high Attack
   → It's the stat most associated with player perception of strength


LIMITATIONS OF THIS CHOICE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We acknowledge this selection is not perfect:

1. Defense, Speed, and HP also affect battle outcomes
   → A slow, fragile Pokémon may never land its powerful attacks
   → Survivability matters for sustained damage

2. Special Attack is equally valid for many Pokémon
   → Some Pokémon are "special attackers" (Alakazam, Gengar)
   → They use Sp.Attack, not Attack, for their main damage

3. The "best" measure of power might be a composite
   → Total Stats (sum of all six stats) captures overall strength
   → But this may mask specific trends in offensive vs defensive design

4. Competitive viability involves more than raw stats
   → Typing, abilities, movepools, and speed tiers matter
   → A statistically weak Pokémon can be competitively strong


OUR APPROACH: PRIMARY TARGET WITH ROBUSTNESS CHECKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Given these considerations, we structure our analysis as:

    PRIMARY TARGET: Attack
    → Most direct measure of offensive power
    → Clear interpretation: "Can this Pokémon hit hard?"

    SECONDARY TARGETS (robustness checks):
    → Total Stats: Overall power across all dimensions
    → Offensive Power (Attack + Sp.Attack): Combined damage potential
    → HP: Survivability trend
    → Speed: Priority/turn order trend

If power creep exists, we expect to see it most clearly in Attack. If we find significant positive trends in Attack AND these trends are confirmed in secondary targets, our evidence for power creep becomes stronger.


================================================================================
WHAT WOULD CONVINCE US?
================================================================================

We establish clear criteria BEFORE analysis:

STRONG EVIDENCE FOR GENERATIONAL DIFFERENCES:
    ✓ Generation is statistically significant (p < 0.05) after controlling 
      for other factors like type and rarity
    ✓ The effect is robust to outlier removal
    ✓ The effect persists among Normal-rarity Pokémon (not just legendaries)
    ✓ Multiple statistical tests agree (regression, ANOVA, non-parametric)

STRONG EVIDENCE SPECIFICALLY FOR POWER CREEP:
    ✓ All of the above, PLUS
    ✓ The direction of the effect is positive (later gens have HIGHER stats)
    ✓ The trend is approximately monotonic (consistent increase over time)
    ✓ The effect is practically meaningful (not just statistically significant)

EVIDENCE AGAINST GENERATIONAL DIFFERENCES:
    ✗ Generation is not significant after controlling for confounds
    ✗ Any apparent effect disappears when outliers are removed
    ✗ The effect is driven entirely by legendary/mythical Pokémon
    ✗ Effect sizes are trivially small


================================================================================
STRUCTURE OF THE ANALYSIS
================================================================================

With this framework established, our analysis proceeds as:

    1. EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS
       → Visualize Attack distribution across generations
       → Check for obvious patterns or anomalies
       → Identify potential confounds (rarity, type distributions)

    2. GLM PREREQUISITES
       → Verify data meets assumptions for our chosen models
       → Justify model specifications

    3. LINEAR MODELS
       → Basic regression: Attack ~ Generation
       → Full regression: Attack ~ Generation + Controls
       → Test if Generation is significant after controlling for confounds

    4. REGULARIZATION
       → LASSO to identify most important predictors
       → Does Generation survive feature selection?

    5. FORMAL HYPOTHESIS TESTS
       → Likelihood Ratio tests for Generation effect
       → Jonckheere-Terpstra test for monotonic trend

    6. ROBUSTNESS CHECKS
       → Outlier-free dataset
       → Normal-only Pokémon (excluding legendaries)
       → Alternative model specifications (Gamma GLM)

    7. SECONDARY ANALYSES
       → Type-specific trends
       → Rarity-specific trends
       → Other target variables (Total Stats, Speed, etc.)

    8. CONCLUSIONS
       → Synthesize evidence for/against generational differences
       → Interpret findings in context of "power creep" narrative
